# Wild cards for Unicorn Egg Nest v3

Brainstorm, 2026-10-12. Lens: surprising ideas a cautious designer would miss. Every idea keeps the v2 core: warm an egg, hatch, name, spell, keep the baby forever. Each one adds something to do with the herd, and reading or maths is how that thing works.

Sources: `docs/v3/research/` (v2-audit, engagement, curriculum, naming, engine). Step codes (R, S, M) refer to the ladders in `curriculum.md`.

## Recommendation

Build these first:

1. **Unicorn School.** Emma corrects the baby, not the other way round. It turns her love of nursing into the strongest learning effect we found (teaching a character), and mistakes stop being hers.
2. **Herd in the Sky.** It is cheap and makes "I have more than 10" into place value. It also gives her names back to her as reading practice.
3. **Visitors from the other games.** Pippa and the Fluffles were her shared second place. Both work as visitors in the unicorn world.
4. **Emma's Moon Castle.** A goal that lasts for months. It is also where most role play happens.

Papa's Market Day is the best way for Tjitse to join in. Try it once as a test before building more.

The ideas with the most risk are the ones that need the real world or a microphone: the Queen's Paper Letters, the Bedtime Story Shell and Post to Oma. They need Tjitse's time and a decision on privacy. Each one is easy to leave out without breaking the others.

## Rules every idea keeps

- Elizabeth is the only voice for letter sounds, words, names and numbers. Babies giggle and say tiny words in the Flicker voice, never learning content. Fluffles only babble.
- All audio is generated ahead of time. New free text (a name, a postcard reply) waits in the generator queue. Until its clip exists, the game sounds it out with the approved letter sounds.
- A wrong answer is a small, funny event in the world, followed by a hint. There is no buzzer, no red cross and no loss.
- No streaks, timers, sick babies, purchases or "come back tomorrow". Missing a week costs nothing.
- Nothing personal leaves the iPad unless Tjitse sends it himself.

## 1. Unicorn School: Emma teaches her babies

**What Emma sees and does.** A little schoolhouse with a slate board and three tiny desks stands at the edge of the meadow. Emma picks two or three babies to bring to class. A baby tries to build a word or a number bond on the slate and gets it a bit wrong. Emma drags the right gem or stone into place. The baby gets it, does its trick and earns a chalk star on its own report card.

**Role play.** Emma is the teacher. Queen Lumi says "Teacher Emma, your class is ready!". Emma rings the school bell and calls babies by name. She picks which baby goes to the slate. At the end, the babies get "graduation ribbons" for the skill they learned.

**Reading.** Elizabeth says the target word: "Bed." The baby puts up b, e, t. Elizabeth reads back exactly what the baby built: "b, e, t... bet?" The baby tilts its head. Emma swaps t for d, and Elizabeth reads "b, e, d, bed!". This trains segmenting and checking her own spelling. It also targets her Dutch slips directly: final d and t (S06), e and a (R11), and the Dutch value of ee and oo (R20). We pick the baby's mistake on purpose to match the step she is on. Later, the baby reads a short sign wrong ("the cat is in the bus") and Emma taps the word that does not match the picture.

**Maths.** The baby puts 4 stones in the moon cups when 3 cups were cold. The egg shows red cheeks again, and Elizabeth says "Too hot! One too many." Emma takes one stone off. Later steps: the baby counts on from the wrong number, writes 42 for "twenty-four" (the Dutch "four and twenty" slip, M20) or counts a pair twice.

**A wrong correction.** If Emma "fixes" it the wrong way, the baby tries her version. Elizabeth reads it back honestly, the baby giggles and scratches its mane, and the next hint comes from the hint ladder. Nobody is in trouble. The baby was the one learning.

**Collecting.** Every baby has a report card in the Nest Book with chalk stars for each skill (letters, words, counting, bonds). Graduation ribbons come in a set of colours, one per skill. Babies with a full card wear a little scholar's cap in the meadow.

**Why she keeps playing.** Each baby has its own progress, so a herd of 30 is 30 small projects. New babies arrive knowing nothing and need her. When she moves up a step, her babies move up with her, so the content of the class changes with her.

**Clarity risk.** Spotting a mistake is harder than making the answer. She may not see that the baby is wrong. To help: the wrong gem wobbles and the baby points at it, and the first lesson is shown with the ghost hand.

**Fit with feedback.** Nursing and caring (v1 favourite), naming (she calls them by name), and "say the word, then she spells it" from Pippa. Research: the protege effect (Chase 2009) and a care-receiving robot that taught 3 to 6 year olds English words (Tanaka 2012).

**Build cost:** M. It reuses the slate tray, the moon cups and the hint ladder. New: the mistake planner and the report cards.

## 2. The Queen's Paper Letters: a treasure hunt in the real house

**What Emma sees and does.** Some nights, a sealed royal envelope lands in the meadow. Inside, the game shows Queen Lumi's letter, one decodable sentence with a picture ("Look in the red box.") and Elizabeth help on each word when Emma taps it. Before that, Tjitse has printed the same letter from the parent corner and hidden a small paper "moon token" in the real red box at home. Emma reads the clue, goes to find it, and the token has a picture code on it (for example, star, moon, star). She enters the code on the Queen's seal, and a rare "paper egg" arrives.

**Role play.** Emma is the Queen's secret messenger. The paper letter has a wax seal printed on it and is addressed to "Nest Keeper Emma".

**Reading.** One or two sentences per letter, made from words on her current reading step and her tricky words (in, the, is, to, under). She has to read for meaning, because the sentence tells her where to go in the real world.

**Maths.** At higher steps the code is a small sum on the token: "3 stars and 4 stars". She sets the moon dial to 7. Or she needs to find 2 tokens, one with 5 dots and one with 5 dots, to make a "ten" token.

**A wrong answer.** If the dial is wrong, the seal stays shut and gives a sleepy yawn. Elizabeth says "Hmm, the seal is still asleep. Count the stars again." If she cannot find the token, Tjitse is there.

**Collecting.** Paper eggs hatch "paper unicorns" with a folded-paper look, a separate set in the Nest Book. The real tokens can go into a real box, and she can keep them.

**Why she keeps playing.** It is rare and different. Real objects at home make it feel like magic. Tjitse decides how often it happens, so it never turns into a chore.

**Clarity risk.** She needs to know that the letter is about the real house. The first letter must be done together with Tjitse. Without a parent it does not work, and the game must never wait on it: it only appears when Tjitse has printed one.

**Fit with feedback.** Royalty, collecting real things, and the Queen's gifts she liked. Research: Hirsh-Pasek's "social" pillar and reading for meaning.

**Build cost:** M. A print layout in the parent corner, the code dial and a paper egg skin.

## 3. Papa's Market Day: co-op role play with a parent

**What Emma sees and does.** Emma opens a market stall in the meadow. Her babies stand behind the counter with ribbons, apples, hats and pins in small baskets. Tjitse sits next to her and plays a customer: "the Duke of Waffles" or any role he likes. He can see a small card in his corner of the screen, in text for adults, with a suggested order: "Ask for 7 apples." Emma counts the apples into the basket, writes the price tag with number gems and takes the coins.

**Role play.** Shopkeeper and customer. She names the stall, chooses which baby helps her that day, and decides what is for sale. Tjitse can be silly. The duke can drop coins, ask for "a hat for my cat" or pay too much.

**Reading.** Price tags and shop signs she builds from gems ("hats", "big hat", "red pin"). The customer card can ask for an item by its word only, so Emma has to read the label in the basket to find it. Elizabeth reads each label when tapped.

**Maths.** Counting out, adding two orders, change from 10 or 20 (M steps on money, with euros or pounds as a setting), and halves ("half of the apples for my horse").

**A wrong answer.** Tjitse reacts in character: "Eight apples! Lovely, one for my hat then." The game counts the basket with chimes so they can both see the number.

**Collecting.** Every new customer leaves a "visitor card" in the shop book (a picture of a funny noble). Coins are not a currency she spends. They go into a jar that fills a picture of the stall, which then gets a new awning.

**Why she keeps playing.** It is play with Papa. He can make it new every time. The stall grows over time.

**Clarity risk.** Low for Emma, because Tjitse is there. The risk is for Tjitse: his card must be short and must suggest an order at her current step. Without a parent the mode stays closed.

**Fit with feedback.** Role play, and Tjitse asked for "more options on what she can do with the unicorns". Research: relatedness, play with a parent, money maths not covered in v2.

**Build cost:** M.

## 4. The Moon Gazette: a photo studio and newspaper

**What Emma sees and does.** A photo studio with backdrops (moon meadow, beach, castle, snowy hill) and a big camera on legs. Emma drags babies into the picture, puts hats and pins on them, and taps the camera. The flash goes off and the photo drops into her album. Sometimes a reporter owl from the Moon Gazette asks for a front-page photo: "Tonight's front page needs three unicorns in hats!". She makes it, and the front page prints with her photo and a headline she builds.

**Role play.** Photographer and stylist. She poses babies, chooses outfits and makes the headline.

**Reading.** She builds a caption from word cards that show a word and a small picture: "Tam" "has" "a" "red" "hat". Elizabeth reads the caption word by word, and the photo checks it. If the caption says "red hat" and the hat is blue, the baby on the photo blinks and changes its hat to red. So her sentence changes the picture. That makes word order and meaning visible, and she can see what her words do.

**Maths.** Photo requests use counts and part-whole: "5 unicorns, 2 with crowns". Later: "more babies with hats than without", "a row of 10, every second one in pink" (counting in twos, patterns, M steps on pattern and comparison).

**A wrong answer.** The owl looks at the photo and says, in Elizabeth's words, "Four unicorns. We need five!" A fifth spot on the stage glows. Or she keeps the photo anyway: it goes in her own album, just not on the front page.

**Collecting.** The album, front pages (one per week, numbered), backdrops and props. Silhouettes show the backdrops still to find.

**Why she keeps playing.** Combinations never run out. Each new item, baby or land gives new photos. She can show the album to Papa. Tjitse can save a front page to the iPad Photos app to send to family.

**Clarity risk.** Building a sentence from cards is new. Start with one word (the name), then two (name and colour), then a short sentence.

**Fit with feedback.** Collecting, naming, dressing up, and "something to show Papa" (engagement principle 19). Endless Reader's "words that do things" idea.

**Build cost:** M. Needs layered art for poses and props, which v3 needs anyway.

## 5. The Bedtime Story Shell: Emma reads to her babies

**What Emma sees and does.** At bedtime, the babies lie in their moss beds and a big pink seashell sits by the fire. Emma opens a tiny picture book: one page, one short sentence and a picture. She presses the shell and reads the page aloud to her babies. The shell records her. When she stops, one baby yawns and falls asleep. Then Elizabeth reads the same page softly. After three pages, all babies are asleep and the night ends.

**Role play.** Emma is the grown-up putting her children to bed. The babies snuggle closer while she reads.

**Reading.** Short decodable pages at her step (later with tricky words and two sentences). She reads first, then hears Elizabeth, which is a model to compare with. She can tap any word for Elizabeth's help before she reads. The book itself is a collected item.

**Maths.** Light. Each page has a small number picture ("5 stars in the sky"), and the babies fall asleep one at a time, so she sees "3 asleep, 2 awake". A sleepy counting song at the end counts down from her herd number. It is not the main point.

**A wrong answer.** There is none. Nothing is checked, and the game cannot hear whether she read it right. The babies fall asleep anyway. This is a toy, not a test.

**Collecting.** The bookshelf: each book she has read gets a spine on the shelf. In the parent corner, Tjitse can listen to her recordings. After months, it is a diary of how her reading changed.

**Why she keeps playing.** It is a calm, fixed end to each night. New books arrive with each land. The voice diary is a reason for Tjitse to keep it.

**Clarity risk.** Low for the action. The microphone needs a Safari permission prompt, which Tjitse must approve once. Recordings stay in IndexedDB on the iPad and are never uploaded. Her voice is never used as the voice for names or words (naming.md).

**Fit with feedback.** Nursing, bedtime from v2, and the engagement principle "a short night with a clean end". Research: Lillard says role play itself does not teach, so here it carries real reading aloud.

**Build cost:** M. Mic recording and a playback page in the parent corner.

## 6. Babies who remember: memory pearls

**What Emma sees and does.** Each baby has a small personality: brave, shy, greedy, sleepy or silly. It shows in how the baby walks and what it likes. Babies remember things she did with them. Some nights, a baby comes to her with a thought bubble that shows a picture from a past night: the pin she gave it. Lumi says "Tam remembers something!" Emma builds the word "pin" from gems to remember it together. Then the baby gives her a memory pearl.

**Role play.** A real friendship with each baby. A shy baby hides and comes out only when she calls its name. A greedy baby steals the apple from the photo set.

**Reading.** This is spaced review in story form. Memory bubbles bring back words, names and tricky words from weeks ago, at the gaps the curriculum file proposes (2, 4, 8 and 16 days). The baby's own name can also come back on its birthday card for her to read.

**Maths.** Baby birthdays: each baby counts its age in nights. "Tam is 9 nights old. Tomorrow Tam is...?" She puts the right candles on the cake. "Who is older, Tam or Bex?" She compares numbers from her own herd. Memory pearls go on a string by baby, and 10 pearls make a necklace, so she sees tens.

**A wrong answer.** If she builds a different word, the bubble goes fuzzy and the baby frowns as if trying to remember: "Hmm, was it a pan? It looked like this." The picture in the bubble becomes clearer and a gem glows as a hint.

**Collecting.** Memory pearls, necklaces per baby, birthday cards in the Nest Book.

**Why she keeps playing.** The babies have a history with her and it grows over months. Old babies stay interesting, which keeps the herd alive beyond the first hatch.

**Clarity risk.** A picture in a thought bubble is abstract for a 6-year-old. The first memory should be from the same night ("Tam remembers your apple from tonight!").

**Fit with feedback.** Naming and owning her babies. Research: retrieval practice and spacing work at her age, and engagement principle 12 ("old content comes back through the herd").

**Build cost:** M to L. Personalities need animation variants. The memory planner is simple once the review schedule exists.

## 7. Visitors from the other games: Pippa and the Fluffles

**What Emma sees and does.** Princess Pippa (Amelia voice) rides into the meadow on a white pony with her word wand. "Hello, Emma! May I make something for your unicorns?" Pippa says the item, Elizabeth says the word, and Emma spells it with the gems on the wand. The item pops out of the wand and goes into the meadow. Another night, a basket of Fluffles (Peanut babble, never real words) rolls in. Each Fluffle holds a written wish card, and the card is the only clue. Emma reads "jam" and gives the jam.

**Role play.** Emma is the host. She welcomes guests, shows them her herd and gives them a tea party. Fluffles can stay over and become a second species she looks after.

**Reading.** Pippa: spelling with a spoken target, exactly the "say the word, then she spells it" she asked for. Fluffles: reading for meaning, because the babble gives nothing away. Later the Fluffle cards become phrases ("a big red jam").

**Maths.** Fluffles bring the v2 ten-frame tummy: "Bobo wants 6 berries" and the plates must fill the frame exactly. Pippa's tea party: set places for every guest, one cup each, how many more cups?

**A wrong answer.** A wrong spell makes the wand give the wrong item, and Pippa laughs: "A pin? For a party? Silly wand!" A wrong gift to a Fluffle gets the v2 funny reaction (balanced on its head, babble giggle).

**Collecting.** Fluffle eggs: Fluffles hatch from round, fuzzy eggs and need a different nest. Pippa's wand items get a page in the Nest Book. Guests leave a signed photo for the Gazette.

**Why she keeps playing.** Visitors come and go, so the world changes without new rules. Each visitor brings its own small mechanic from a game she already liked.

**Clarity risk.** Low, because she knows both. Pippa's v2 problem was "unclear which word is wanted": Elizabeth must say the word, and Pippa must hold up a picture of it.

**Fit with feedback.** Pippa and Fluffle Nursery shared second place. The sticker book and caring for fluffy animals were named as reasons. Reuses two working demos.

**Build cost:** M. The mechanics and voices exist. Pippa and the Fluffles need new art in the v3 style.

## 8. Emma's Moon Castle: something she builds for months

**What Emma sees and does.** At first, an empty hill with a single stone. Over weeks, a castle grows there. Every few nights, the Queen's builders (small badgers in hard hats) ask Emma what to build next and show two or three picture cards: a stable, a ballroom, a library, a nursery, a tower. Emma picks one. Then she builds it: she stacks blocks of 10 and single bricks to make the wall, chooses the colour, and moves the furniture in. Her babies move into their rooms.

**Role play.** Princess of her own castle. Each room is a place for role play: babies sleep in the nursery, dance in the ballroom, eat in the kitchen and read in the library. Royal events (a ball, a birthday) happen in the rooms she built.

**Reading.** Every room gets a sign she builds ("stable", "bath", "ballroom" at later steps). The library shelves fill with the books she has read (see idea 5). Invitations to a ball are short sentences she reads to find out which baby is invited.

**Maths.** The strongest maths carrier. A wall of 34 bricks is 3 tens and 4 ones (place value to 100, M25 to M30). The stable door must be taller than Tam, so she measures babies in bricks (length and height). The ballroom floor is tiled in a pattern she continues. The flag is cut in halves and quarters. Tables in the hall seat 2, 5 or 10 babies each (times tables as rows).

**A wrong answer.** If the wall is too short, the badger stands on a ladder and the gap shows clearly in the sky: "Three bricks more, please!" If it is too tall, the top brick wobbles and the badgers giggle as it falls into their wheelbarrow.

**Collecting.** Rooms, furniture sets, flags, banners. Each room has a page with silhouettes of the furniture still to find.

**Why she keeps playing.** It is a long goal she can see. It is different every time, because she chose the rooms. It gives a reason to keep doing maths after bonds to 10. Tjitse can see it grow too.

**Clarity risk.** Building tools can be complicated. Keep each build to one action ("fill the wall") with a picture of the goal. Never offer more than three room choices.

**Fit with feedback.** Royalty, role play, collecting and "an actual game with levels". Research: Animal Crossing and Toca (something that grows and lasts), engagement principle 13 (levels as places).

**Build cost:** L. It needs a lot of art and a save model for rooms.

## 9. Herd in the Sky: her babies become stars

**What Emma sees and does.** At bedtime, the camera tilts up to the night sky. Every baby she has hatched has its own star, labelled with its name. Every ten stars join into a constellation that makes a unicorn shape. With 14 babies, she sees one full constellation and four loose stars. When the eleventh baby hatches, its star flies up and the first constellation lights its lines. Lumi says "Ten babies make a unicorn in the sky! One ten and one more."

**Role play.** Emma is the keeper of the sky. She can name each constellation, just as she names babies.

**Reading.** Wishing on a star. Lumi says "Make a wish on Bex's star!" and Emma finds the star whose label reads "Bex" among four or five names from her own herd. Names are made-up words, so this is the pseudo-word reading that the Year 1 phonics check tests. Elizabeth says the name when she taps a star, so she can check herself.

**Maths.** Place value from her own herd: tens as constellations, ones as loose stars. "You have 14 unicorns!" is shown as one unicorn shape and four stars. She counts on from 10 instead of from 1. Later: "How many more babies for the next sky unicorn?" (bonds to the next ten), and by the time she reaches 30, counting in tens.

**A wrong answer.** If she taps the wrong star, that star twinkles and Elizabeth says its name: "That's Tam. Bex is shy tonight." The right star twinkles once as a hint.

**Collecting.** Constellations (one per ten babies), each with a picture and the name she chose. Shooting stars on first-try nights.

**Why she keeps playing.** It shows how big her herd is, which is what she was proud of. It grows with every baby. The next constellation is always visible as an outline.

**Clarity risk.** Low. The sky is a picture of what she owns. The tens idea must be said, not just shown.

**Fit with feedback.** "She hatched more than 10 eggs and was really proud." Research: young collectors care about quantity, and engagement principle 4 ("show what she owns and use it as maths").

**Build cost:** S. One scene, star placement, and names that already exist.

## 10. Post to Oma: postcards to family

**What Emma sees and does.** A little post office with a red post box and a snail in a postal cap. Emma picks a photo from her album (idea 4) or a baby, and builds a short postcard text from word cards: "Tam can hop." "I have 12 unicorns." Elizabeth reads it back. She chooses a stamp, puts the card in the post box and the snail carries it off. Tjitse can then share the postcard as a picture to Oma and Opa in the Netherlands from the parent corner. When a family member replies, Tjitse types the reply in the parent corner. The parent corner only accepts words from her current reading list. After the next generator run, the reply arrives in the post box as a letter, which Elizabeth can read word by word.

**Role play.** A real pen pal, with her unicorns as the topic. The snail postman is a regular character.

**Reading.** Writing sentences (composition), then reading a reply that is about her. Replies use her words at her step, so the letter is readable and personal.

**Maths.** Stamps: a postcard to the Netherlands costs 5 moon stamps. She combines a 2 and a 3 stamp. Later: euros and cents for stamps, if money is in her steps.

**A wrong answer.** There are no wrong postcards. If a sentence does not make sense ("hop can Tam"), Elizabeth reads it as it is, the snail scratches its head, and she can move the cards. It is sent either way.

**Collecting.** Stamps (one design per land), replies in a letter box, the postcards she sent.

**Why she keeps playing.** Real replies from people she loves, in English. It brings her family into her English learning, even though they speak Dutch at home.

**Clarity risk.** High for the reply step. She will not understand the delay before a reply can be read. Tjitse must handle the parent corner and the generator. This only works if Tjitse wants that work.

**Fit with feedback.** Family and relatedness. "Something to show Papa", and also Oma. Uses the same "generate later" queue that naming.md proposes for new names.

**Build cost:** M to L. The postcard export is simple. The reply flow needs the parent corner, a word-list check and the generator queue.
