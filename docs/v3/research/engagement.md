# What keeps a 6-year-old playing for months

Research for v3 of Unicorn Egg Nest. Date: 2026-10-12.

## Answer

Children keep coming back to a game when it gives them things that are theirs (a named creature, a collection that grows), things to do with them that they choose themselves, and a challenge that is just hard enough. The research behind this is reasonably solid: self-determination theory (autonomy, competence, relatedness), the value of choice for children, and learning that is part of the game action rather than a quiz between game turns. Rewards that are announced as payment for a task can lower interest. Surprise rewards and rewards that are part of the story do not.

The long-running children's titles that families do not regret share a pattern. Teach Your Monster to Read, Reading Eggs, Animal Crossing, Pokemon and Toca Life all give the child a creature, avatar or world they own, a collection with visible size, and new content in small doses. The titles that hold children through pressure (streak loss, sick or dying pets, countdowns, characters that cry, items locked behind payment) are the dark patterns that Radesky and colleagues found in four out of five apps used by 3 to 5 year olds. We use the first list and none of the second.

Emma's own feedback already points in the same direction. She was proud of hatching more than ten babies, she loved naming them, and she loved collecting items such as the pins. She did not like being told "I don't know this name". The 20 principles at the end turn this into rules for v3.

## Evidence: what is well supported, what is not

Strength labels:
- **Strong:** several studies or a meta-analysis, effect replicated, including children.
- **Moderate:** a few good studies, small samples, or older children than Emma.
- **Weak:** single study, correlational, vendor claims or expert opinion.

I read abstracts, reviews and reliable secondary summaries, not every full text. Where a detail comes from my own background knowledge and not from a source I checked, it says so.

### Motivation

| Claim | Evidence | Strength | For us |
|---|---|---|---|
| Games pull people back when they satisfy autonomy, competence and relatedness. | Ryan, Rigby and Przybylski (2006), four studies: each need independently predicted enjoyment and intention to play again. Samples were mostly older players. | Strong for the general model, moderate for young children. | Every feature should give her a choice, a success she can see, or a bond (with a baby, Queen Lumi or Papa). |
| Choice raises motivation, effort and performance, more in children than adults. | Patall, Cooper and Robinson (2008), meta-analysis of 41 studies. Stronger effects for children, for 2 to 4 successive choices, for choices that do not change the learning content, and when no reward followed the choice. | Strong. | Offer small, frequent, low-stakes choices (which egg, which baby, which ribbon). Do not hand her a menu of twenty. |
| Expected, tangible rewards for doing a task lower later free-choice interest. Unexpected rewards and informational feedback do not. | Lepper, Greene and Nisbett (1973): preschoolers promised a "Good Player" award drew less afterwards. Those given the same award by surprise did not. Deci, Koestner and Ryan (1999), meta-analysis: expected, tangible, task-contingent rewards undermine intrinsic motivation, more so for children. Cameron and Pierce dispute the size. | Strong for the direction, debated on size. | Never say "read this to get a sticker". Rewards appear as part of the story or as surprises. Verbal feedback that tells her what she did well is safe. |
| Learning that is the core game action beats learning stuck between game turns. | Habgood and Ainsworth (2011), Zombie Division, 7 to 11 year olds: the intrinsic version produced more learning in fixed time, and children chose to play it 7 times longer when free to pick. A 2022 field study warns that more time on task does not by itself mean more learning. | Moderate (small samples, older children). | Warming the egg with number stones and spelling the name are the right model. Avoid "answer a question, then you may play". |
| Fantasy context, personalisation and incidental choice raise both engagement and learning. | Cordova and Lepper (1996), Journal of Educational Psychology. I could only confirm the citation and title. The result as I know it from background: children in a maths game set in a story, with their own and friends' names and with choices about cosmetic details, learned more and chose harder problems. Malone and Lepper (1987) list challenge, curiosity, control and fantasy as the core motivators. | Moderate. | The fantasy, her name for each baby and her cosmetic choices are not decoration. They are part of why she will learn. |
| Praise for the act works better than praise for the person. | Mueller and Dweck (1998): fifth graders praised for intelligence later avoided challenge more than those praised for effort. Cimpian and colleagues (2007): with young children, "you did a good job drawing" protected motivation after a mistake better than "you are a good drawer" (detail from background knowledge). Some growth-mindset work has replicated weakly. | Moderate. | Lumi says "You warmed every cup!" and "You found all the sounds!", not "You're so clever!". |
| Children work harder when they are teaching someone else (the protege effect). | Chase, Chin, Oppezzo and Schwartz (2009): students who believed they were teaching their own agent spent more time and learned more, most for low achievers. Mistakes felt less personal. Tanaka and Matsuzoe (2012): Japanese 3 to 6 year olds learned English verbs by teaching a care-receiving robot. | Moderate. | Let Emma teach her unicorns. A baby that "gets it wrong" lets her correct it without her own failure. This fits her love of nursing. |
| Children learn very many distinct named things inside a collection they care about. | Balmford and colleagues (2002, Science): UK 8 year olds named about 78% of Pokemon and about 53% of common wildlife. | Weak to moderate (one survey, correlational). | A collection of distinct, named individuals can carry a lot of content (names, words, numbers). |

### Learning

| Claim | Evidence | Strength | For us |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mastery learning (move on when secure, extra practice when not) raises results. | Kulik, Kulik and Bangert-Drowns (1990), 108 studies, average effect about 0.5 SD with large spread. Intelligent tutoring systems in K-12 about 0.4 (Kulik and Fletcher 2016). | Strong. | Keep the per-strand staircases from v2. Move up on first-try success, down quietly on help. |
| Retrieval practice (recalling, not re-seeing) helps young children remember. | Robust testing effects in first and third graders, with no difference by age. Preschool effects depend on cued recall and feedback. | Strong for age 6 to 8 in lab tasks, thinner in classrooms. | Old names, words and number bonds should come back for her to recall, with a cue (a picture, the baby). |
| Spreading practice over days beats massing it. | Strong in adults and older children. Classroom studies with children are mixed (some null results when the massed condition was itself spread out). | Moderate for Emma's age. | Revisit content across nights through the herd. Do not drill one word ten times in one night. |
| Building words from sounds (invented spelling) helps learning to read. | Senechal, Ouellette, Pagan and Lever (2012): children with weak phoneme awareness trained in invented spelling learned to read more words than those in phoneme segmentation or storybook groups. Ouellette and Senechal (2008, 2017) link invented spelling to later reading. | Moderate. | Strong support for free name building, with an honest read-back of what she built. Refusing her name throws this away. |
| A good learning app is minds-on, engaging without distraction, meaningful and social. | Hirsh-Pasek and colleagues (2015), "Putting Education in Educational Apps". This is a framework from the science of learning, not a trial. | Moderate (expert synthesis). | Taps must be decisions, not busywork. Every word should mean something in the world (a pin she can see). Leave room for Papa to play along. |

### Play, age and gender

| Claim | Evidence | Strength | For us |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pretend play is a crucial cause of development. | Lillard and colleagues (2013), review: no good evidence that pretend play causes creativity, intelligence or problem solving. Possible links to language, storytelling, social development and self-regulation. Critics say the review was too negative. | The causal claim is weak. | Role play is justified because Emma loves it and it carries the learning, not as a learning method in its own right. Spoken stories around the babies may help her English. |
| Around 6 to 7, games with rules become common alongside pretend play. | Smith and Pellegrini (2013): games with rules common after about 6. | Moderate. | Emma is at the switch. Levels, goals and collections (rules) plus free care play (pretend) suit her. The demo's success with both confirms it. |
| Pretend play engagement within one session peaks around 10 to 15 minutes. | One 2025 study of 93 children aged 3 to 6. | Weak. | A night of about 10 to 15 minutes is a sensible target, with a natural end. |
| Young children collect for quantity, older children for value and social reasons. | A report of University of Nebraska work: 5 to 7 focus on quantity. A 2017 focus group study: under-7s collect for play, older children for peer status. | Weak. | Her pride in "more than 10" fits. Show the count, big and often. |
| Girls and boys differ in toy preferences on average. | Davis and Hines (2020), meta-analysis of 75 studies: large and stable group differences. DeLoache, Simcock and Macari (2007): girls' intense interests more often dolls and dress-up. Brenda Laurel's Purple Moon research (1990s, market research): girls drawn to character stories and relationships. | Strong at group level, weak for any one child. | Use only as a weak hint. Emma's own stated likes (unicorns, horses, royalty, care, Furby-like creatures) and her verdicts decide. |

### Engagement over months and dark patterns

| Claim | Evidence | Strength | For us |
|---|---|---|---|
| Novelty wears off. | Novelty effects in learning technology are widely reported. A 14-week gamification study found effects dropped after about 4 weeks and partly recovered later. No child-specific meta-analysis found. | Moderate in general, weak for children specifically. | Plan new content in small, regular doses across months, not all at once. |
| Most apps for young children use manipulative design. | Radesky and colleagues (2022, JAMA Network Open), apps used by 160 children aged 3 to 5: four in five apps used manipulative design. 99% of children had at least one in their top apps. Types: prolonging play ("Don't just stand there!"), countdown timers, return prompts ("Come back tomorrow and get a dragon"), purchase pressure, characters who cry if the child refuses, accumulating daily rewards, unclosable ads. | Strong (good descriptive study). | A list of things we never do. |
| Neglect penalties drive anxiety more than joy. | Tamagotchi: a pet could die within half a day; Bandai added a pause after complaints; schools banned it. Webkinz: one parent account of a child quitting after rumours that pets die. Mostly press and anecdote. | Weak (no controlled studies), but consistent. | Babies never get sick, sad, lost or die. Absence has no cost. |
| Chance-based paid rewards are linked to problem gambling. | Zendle and colleagues (2020): loot box spending correlates with problem gambling. UK committee: chance-based loot boxes should not be sold to children. Correlational. | Moderate. | No paid anything. Random surprises stay small, free and never the main reason to play. |
| Session length for learning apps. | No research standard found. Vendors: GraphoGame says 15 minutes, 3 times a week. Numbots says 3 minutes a day, 4 to 5 days a week. AAP for 6+: consistent limits set by the family, media not displacing sleep and activity. | Weak. | A 10 to 15 minute night, 3 to 5 nights a week, set by Tjitse. The game ends the night itself. |

## What is well supported, what is opinion

**Well supported**
- Satisfying autonomy, competence and relatedness predicts enjoyment and return play.
- Choice helps children's motivation, best in small numbers.
- Expected, tangible rewards for a task can lower interest. Surprises and informative feedback do not.
- Mastery-based progression and retrieval practice help learning at Emma's age.
- Manipulative design is common in young children's apps, and we can name its forms.

**Reasonably supported, with limits**
- Learning built into the core game action keeps children playing longer and learning more (small samples, older children).
- Fantasy and personalisation raise engagement and learning.
- Teaching a character increases effort (protege effect).
- Invented spelling supports learning to read.
- Praising the act protects motivation after mistakes.
- Spacing helps, but classroom child studies are mixed.

**Weak or opinion**
- That pretend play itself causes learning.
- Exact session lengths, daily rituals and novelty timing.
- Why specific commercial titles last. Nearly all of it is design commentary, vendor claims or anecdote. Teach Your Monster, Reading Eggs, Endless Reader and Duolingo ABC have no independent long-term studies that I found. GraphoGame has some RCT evidence.
- Girls' preferences, which hold at group level only. Emma's own verdicts are the real data.

## Design principles for v3

Each principle names the evidence and the Emma feedback behind it.

1. **The learning is the verb of the fantasy.** Reading and maths are how she warms eggs, names babies, grants wishes and finds them at bedtime. Never a question before the fun. *Evidence:* intrinsic integration, Hirsh-Pasek's minds-on pillar. *Emma:* she loved the egg loop. Moonbeam failed because it had no real maths or reading.

2. **Every baby is a named individual she keeps forever.** Each has a name, look, traits, a growth stage and its own page. Nothing is ever taken away. *Evidence:* ownership and personalisation, collections of distinct individuals (Pokemon), Reading Eggs critters. *Emma:* hatching more than ten and being proud of it.

3. **Never refuse her name.** Any name she builds from her gems is accepted. The game reads it back honestly from the sounds she placed, the baby celebrates, and the tag shows what she wrote. A whole-word Elizabeth clip for a new name can be generated in the next batch and appear later. Only a short blocklist of rude or real-word clashes is greyed out in the tray before she places a gem, so she never gets a "no" after building. *Evidence:* invented spelling, autonomy, personalisation. *Emma:* "I don't know this name" was the one clear dislike.

4. **Show the size of what she owns, and use it as maths.** A big herd count, a Nest Book with numbered pages, sets of items with empty spaces. "You have 14 unicorns!" Counting, comparing and adding her own herd is maths she cares about. *Evidence:* young collectors value quantity (weak), meaningful maths (Hirsh-Pasek). *Emma:* proud of "more than 10".

5. **More things to do with the unicorns, each a toy first.** Grooming, dressing, feeding, bathing, a vet corner, a parade, a royal ball, a school. Each is free play with no wrong answer, like Toca. Learning hooks sit inside some of them (reading a ribbon colour word, counting carrots for a party) and are optional in free play. *Evidence:* autonomy, digital toys, games-with-rules and pretend play overlap at 6. *Tjitse:* she loves role play and needs more options.

6. **Let her teach her babies.** A baby goes to "unicorn school" and learns the letters and numbers Emma shows it. When a baby makes a mistake, Emma fixes it. The baby's skills grow with hers. *Evidence:* protege effect, care-receiving robot. *Emma:* nursing and caring were a v1 favourite.

7. **Rewards are surprises in the world, not wages.** No "read three words to earn a sticker". Items and babies arrive as part of the story: a wish granted leaves the item with the baby, the Queen sends a gift when the star jar fills on its own. Shiny babies are a surprise for first-try success, never promised. No currency to spend. *Evidence:* overjustification, Neopets inflation. *Emma:* she valued the pins as things her babies have, not as points.

8. **Collections with visible gaps.** Item sets (pins, ribbons, crowns, saddles), egg kinds, coat colours, growth stages. Each set has a page with silhouettes of what is still missing. Completing a set makes something happen in the world (a full pin set means the babies can hold a parade). *Evidence:* collection drive (Pokemon, Reading Eggs, Animal Crossing museum). *Emma:* sticker book and pins.

9. **Small choices, often.** Two or three meaningful choices per night: which egg, which baby to play with, which activity, which outfit. Never a long menu. *Evidence:* Patall meta-analysis, strongest for children with 2 to 4 choices.

10. **Real challenge, adapted silently.** Per-strand mastery staircases that move up on first-try success and down on help, without announcement. The golden egg stays as the challenge she chooses herself. Never fake difficulty, never time pressure. *Evidence:* mastery learning, competence need. *Emma:* Moonbeam was too easy. Queen Emma's Letters was too unclear.

11. **One clear goal per screen, spoken and shown.** Lumi's moonbeam, a picture of the goal, an instruction she can replay. New activities get a first-time demo by the ghost hand. *Evidence:* competence needs clear goals. *Emma:* Queen Emma's Letters failed on clarity alone.

12. **Old content comes back through the herd.** Older babies bring wishes with words she learned weeks ago. Bedtime signs use names from the whole herd. Number bonds return with new egg kinds. Recall, with a cue, across nights. *Evidence:* retrieval practice, spacing.

13. **Levels are places in the world.** The Moon Meadow first, then new lands (a crystal lake, a rainbow forest, the Queen's castle) that open as her skills grow. Each land brings new egg kinds, items and baby looks, and matches a phonics phase or maths stage. A map shows where she has been. *Evidence:* Teach Your Monster stages, Reading Eggs map, mastery. *Tjitse:* an actual game with levels.

14. **New things in small doses, for months.** A new egg kind, visitor or item set every week or two, seasonal touches tied to the real calendar (a snowy meadow in winter, a Cyprus summer beach). Plan the content calendar so the herd always has a next thing to look forward to. Nothing expires or is lost if missed. *Evidence:* novelty fades (moderate). Animal Crossing seasons.

15. **A short night with a natural end.** About 10 to 15 minutes, ending with bedtime and goodnight. "One more egg" is offered at most once and never pushed. The game ends cleanly so stopping feels like finishing, not quitting. *Evidence:* session engagement peak (weak), AAP consistent limits, vendor guidance. *Persona:* short sessions, always end with a reward.

16. **No pressure, no guilt, ever.** No streaks, no daily login gifts, no countdowns, no "come back tomorrow" prompts, no characters that cry or beg, no sick, sad, hungry or lost babies, no purchases, no ads, no paid or chance-based boxes. Absence costs nothing. The herd is happy to see her. *Evidence:* Radesky 2022 dark pattern types, ICO children's code, Tamagotchi and Webkinz anecdotes, loot box research.

17. **Praise what she did.** "You warmed every cup!", "You found every sound in Rosie!". Not "You're so clever". Mistakes get a funny, kind world reaction and a hint, never a buzzer. *Evidence:* process praise (Mueller and Dweck, Cimpian). *Emma:* the v2 funny wrong-give reactions.

18. **Babies grow, and so does she.** Babies move through growth stages and learn tricks. The Nest Book also shows her own progress as a picture she can understand: gems for letters she knows, stones for bonds she knows. She sees she is getting better. *Evidence:* competence need, Numbots' robot that upgrades.

19. **Something to show Papa.** A parade or "show my herd" mode where she leads Tjitse through her babies and their items, and Lumi reads their names. Play with a parent is a learning pillar and makes the game part of family life. *Evidence:* relatedness, Hirsh-Pasek's social pillar, AAP co-use advice.

20. **Her verdicts beat our theories and stereotypes.** Unicorns, horses, royalty, care and collecting are her choices, not assumptions about girls. Each new land or activity is tested with her as a demo first, and what she ignores is cut. *Evidence:* group-level gender findings are weak for one child. *Emma:* v1 and v2 verdicts already overturned several of our assumptions.
